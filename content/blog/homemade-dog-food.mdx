# Homemade Dog Food Calorie Calculator & Recipes

Homemade dog food is growing rapidly in popularity as dog owners seek more control over ingredients and transparency about what their pets eat. But home cooking comes with a critical responsibility: getting the calories and complete nutrition right. This guide explains how to calculate the calorie content of any homemade recipe and how to ensure it meets your dog's daily needs.

## Why Calories Matter in Homemade Dog Food

Commercial dog food has a standardised calorie density printed on the packaging. Homemade food does not. Without knowing the calorie content of your recipe, you cannot determine whether you are feeding your dog the right amount regardless of how nutritious the ingredients are.

Most homemade dog food recipes contain between 100 and 180 kcal per 100g depending on the fat content of the protein source and the overall ingredient ratio. A chicken and vegetable recipe will have a very different calorie density than a beef and sweet potato recipe even if both look like similar volumes.

Getting portions wrong over weeks and months leads to gradual weight gain or loss that is difficult to attribute to the diet without careful calorie tracking.

## How to Calculate Calories in Homemade Dog Food

Use the modified Atwater system — the standard method for calculating food energy in both human and veterinary nutrition:

| Macronutrient | Kcal per gram |
|--------------|--------------|
| Protein | 3.5 kcal per gram |
| Fat | 8.5 kcal per gram |
| Carbohydrate | 3.5 kcal per gram |
| Fibre | 0 kcal per gram |

Note that the veterinary Atwater values (3.5 for protein and carbohydrate, 8.5 for fat) differ slightly from human food values (4.0, 4.0, 9.0) to account for the lower digestibility of typical pet food ingredients.

## Step-by-Step Calculation Method

Step 1: Weigh each ingredient in grams before cooking.

Step 2: Look up the protein, fat, and carbohydrate content per 100g for each ingredient using the USDA FoodData Central database (fdc.nal.usda.gov), which is free and comprehensive.

Step 3: Calculate the actual macros for the amount you are using:
Macro amount = (ingredient weight in grams divided by 100) x macro per 100g

Step 4: Apply veterinary Atwater values:
Protein kcal = protein grams x 3.5
Fat kcal = fat grams x 8.5
Carb kcal = carbohydrate grams x 3.5

Step 5: Sum all ingredients for total recipe calories.

Step 6: Divide by batch weight in grams to get kcal per gram, then multiply by 100 for kcal per 100g.

## Example Recipe Calculation

Simple Chicken and Rice Recipe making approximately 4 servings:

| Ingredient | Amount | Protein | Fat | Carbs | Kcal |
|------------|--------|---------|-----|-------|------|
| Chicken breast cooked | 400g | 84g | 8g | 0g | 362 kcal |
| Brown rice cooked | 300g | 8g | 2g | 64g | 308 kcal |
| Carrots raw | 100g | 1g | 0g | 10g | 38 kcal |
| Spinach raw | 50g | 1g | 0g | 2g | 12 kcal |
| Olive oil | 10g | 0g | 10g | 0g | 85 kcal |
| Total | 860g | 94g | 20g | 76g | 805 kcal |

Kcal per 100g: (805 divided by 860) x 100 = 93.6 kcal per 100g

Per 215g serving: approximately 201 kcal

## How Much Homemade Food to Feed Per Day

Once you know the kcal per 100g of your recipe, use this formula to calculate daily portions:

Grams per day = (Dog's daily kcal target divided by Recipe kcal per 100g) x 100

Example: Dog needs 800 kcal per day. Recipe provides 93.6 kcal per 100g.
(800 divided by 93.6) x 100 = 854g per day

Split across 2 meals: 427g per meal

## Nutritional Balance Beyond Calories

Calories are only part of the picture. Homemade dog food must also provide the correct balance of essential nutrients. This is where most homemade diets fail without professional formulation.

Calcium and phosphorus balance is critical for bone health. Muscle meat is naturally very high in phosphorus and almost completely lacking in calcium. Without adding a calcium source — typically raw meaty bones or a calcium supplement — homemade diets cause progressive bone demineralisation.

Essential fatty acids, particularly omega-3 fatty acids from fish or fish oil, must be included. Chicken and beef based diets are typically very high in omega-6 but low in omega-3, leading to inflammatory imbalance over time.

Vitamin deficiencies develop slowly and are difficult to detect without blood testing. Vitamin D, B12, zinc, iodine, and iron are the most commonly deficient nutrients in home-cooked dog diets.

## Simple Balanced Recipe Framework

This framework provides a starting structure for an adult maintenance diet. It must be supplemented with a complete canine vitamin and mineral supplement to meet AAFCO nutritional standards:

- 50 percent lean protein: chicken breast, turkey, lean beef, white fish, or eggs
- 25 percent complex carbohydrates: cooked brown rice, sweet potato, oats, or quinoa
- 15 percent vegetables: carrots, broccoli, spinach, green beans, or zucchini
- 10 percent healthy fat: salmon oil, olive oil, or naturally fatty fish

Add a complete AAFCO-compliant canine vitamin and mineral supplement at the manufacturer's recommended dose for your dog's weight. Without this, the diet will be deficient in multiple essential nutrients regardless of ingredient quality.

## When to Consult a Veterinary Nutritionist

Always consult a board-certified veterinary nutritionist (Diplomate ACVN) before switching to a homemade diet as the primary food source if your dog has any chronic health condition. Kidney disease, liver disease, heart disease, diabetes, inflammatory bowel disease, and food allergies all require specific nutritional modifications that cannot be achieved with generic recipes.

Puppies, pregnant dogs, and nursing dogs have higher and more specific nutritional requirements that make homemade feeding significantly more challenging to balance correctly.

The cost of a single consultation with a veterinary nutritionist — typically between 200 and 400 dollars — is far less than the cost of treating nutritional deficiency diseases that develop over months or years of an improperly balanced diet.
