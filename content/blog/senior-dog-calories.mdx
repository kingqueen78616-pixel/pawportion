# Senior Dog Calorie Needs: When and How to Feed Less

As dogs age, their metabolism slows, their activity level decreases, and their nutritional needs shift in important ways. Feeding a senior dog the same amount as a young adult leads to gradual but significant weight gain — which accelerates joint deterioration, heart disease, diabetes, and other age-related conditions.

This guide explains when to reduce your dog's calories, by how much, and how to do it safely and gradually.

## When Does a Dog Become Senior?

The answer depends on breed size because larger breeds age faster:

| Breed Size | Weight Range | Senior Age |
|------------|-------------|-----------|
| Toy and Small | Under 10 kg | 10 to 12 years |
| Medium | 10 to 25 kg | 8 to 10 years |
| Large | 25 to 45 kg | 7 to 8 years |
| Giant | Over 45 kg | 5 to 6 years |

A Great Dane is considered senior at 5 to 6 years — the same age a Chihuahua is still considered middle-aged. This is because larger bodies work harder to maintain mass, leading to faster cellular ageing.

## How Much Do Calorie Needs Decrease?

Using the vet-standard RER and MER formula, senior dogs use a multiplier of 1.4 compared to 1.6 for standard neutered adults. This represents approximately a 12.5 percent reduction in daily calories.

| Age Stage | Weight | Factor | Daily kcal |
|-----------|--------|--------|-----------|
| Adult 3 years | 20 kg | 1.6 | 1,059 kcal |
| Senior 9 years | 20 kg | 1.4 | 927 kcal |
| Difference | | | minus 132 kcal |

That 132 kcal difference — if not accounted for — leads to gradual fat accumulation of approximately 5 to 7 kg per year in a medium-sized dog. Over 3 years, this represents significant excess weight that stresses joints and organs.

## Signs Your Senior Dog Needs Fewer Calories

Watch for these early warning signs that calorie intake exceeds needs:

Weight gain without any dietary change is the clearest signal. If your dog is gaining weight on the same food and portions they have always had, their metabolism has slowed and portions need reducing.

Reduced activity and shorter walks mean fewer calories are being burned. A dog that used to walk 45 minutes and now manages 20 minutes needs a corresponding reduction in food intake.

Visible fat deposits around the ribcage, tail base, or neck indicate excess body fat accumulation that requires dietary intervention.

Difficulty rising, stiffness after rest, or reluctance to use stairs are signs of joint stress that is often worsened by excess weight. Weight reduction alone frequently produces significant improvements in mobility.

## How to Reduce Senior Dog Calories Safely

### Step 1: Recalculate Using the Senior Factor

Use the formula with the 1.4 senior multiplier:
MER = 70 x (weight in kg)^0.75 x 1.4

Compare this to your dog's current intake. If they are eating more than this, reduce gradually.

### Step 2: Reduce Gradually Over Several Weeks

Never cut calories suddenly. Reduce by approximately 10 percent per week until you reach the calculated target. Sudden reductions cause digestive upset, stress, and compensatory behaviour such as increased food seeking.

### Step 3: Switch to Senior Formula Food

Senior dog foods are typically lower in calories and fat, higher in dietary fibre, and often contain joint-supporting ingredients such as glucosamine and chondroitin. The lower calorie density makes it easier to feed a satisfying volume of food without exceeding the calorie target.

Read the label carefully. Some foods marketed as senior formulas are not significantly different from adult formulas. Look for a meaningful reduction in fat content (ideally below 12 percent on a dry matter basis) and higher fibre content.

### Step 4: Reduce and Replace Treats

Treats become proportionally more significant as total daily calorie needs decrease. A 50 kcal treat is 5 percent of an 854 kcal adult budget but nearly 7 percent of a 720 kcal senior budget.

Switch to low-calorie treat alternatives: raw baby carrots (4 kcal each), apple slices without seeds (5 kcal each), plain cooked green beans (2 kcal each), or blueberries (1 kcal each). These satisfy the reward behaviour without meaningfully impacting calorie balance.

### Step 5: Monitor Weight Monthly

Weigh your senior dog every month. Use the same scale at the same time of day. The goal is stable weight within their ideal range with a BCS of 4 to 5 out of 9.

If weight continues to increase despite calorie reduction, consult your veterinarian to rule out hypothyroidism, Cushing's disease, or other metabolic conditions that commonly develop in senior dogs.

## Senior Dogs Who Need More Calories

Not all senior dogs need fewer calories. Underweight seniors — particularly those with cancer, chronic kidney disease, dental disease, or inflammatory bowel disease — may need increased calories and often require higher protein to maintain muscle mass.

Cancer in particular dramatically increases calorie needs because tumours compete directly with healthy tissue for energy. Senior dogs with cancer may need 1.5 to 2 times their normal MER and benefit from high-fat, high-protein, low-carbohydrate diets.

Always work with your veterinarian if your senior dog is losing weight unintentionally. Unexplained weight loss in a senior dog always warrants investigation.
